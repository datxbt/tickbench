What a Round Turn Costs

Stage 0 priced a trade as spread plus commission. That quietly sets slippage to zero. Measuring it instead moves gold from the cheapest instrument on the book to the dearest — and none of the arithmetic changed.

Trailing 3 months to 2026-08-31 · 696M ticks, 9.4M one-minute bars
Slippage = 0.5 × measured mid drift over a 250 ms fill latency. That fraction is an assumption about the strategy, not a measurement. Every figure below moves with it, and the band is shown.

Decomposition

Three components, one of them assumed

Commission is published, so it is arithmetic. Spread is observed, so each bar carries the spread that was actually quoted during it. Slippage had to be measured off the tape: anchor a tick, read the prevailing mid 250 ms later, take the absolute difference.

Bars are drawn to a common scale in basis points of price, which is the only unit that compares an index to a currency pair. A pip does not.

The reversal

The ranking depends entirely on an assumption

How much of the measured drift is adverse depends on the strategy, not on the tape. A breakout order chases the move and eats most of it; a passive entry need not. So the honest output is a band, and the band is wide enough to reorder the instruments.

All four still sit inside a 1.3× band at the default assumption, so Stage 0's broader conclusion survives: cost is not the reason to prefer one instrument. But any argument that named gold as the cheapest thing to trade was an artifact of leaving slippage out. Setting the fraction to zero is a claim about the strategy, not a saving.

Why the drift had to be measured rather than derived

Square-root-of-time scaling from one-minute bar range is the obvious shortcut, and it underestimates real 250 ms drift by 1.6× on EURUSD and 2.7× on USTEC. Microstructure noise at that horizon never accumulates into a one-minute range. So the profile is sampled from the tape at five fixed horizons, and a latency that was not measured is refused rather than interpolated.

Hour of day

The rollover hour costs six times the cheapest one

Cost is not spread evenly across the day. Each bar is one UTC hour, drawn as a multiple of that symbol's own cheapest hour, so the four strips compare directly. Hatched slots are hours with no quotes at all.

Windows

Cost is not constant across the splits

Spreads have compressed steadily since 2020. A backtest run on the dev split with today's costs is flattered by more than most edges are worth, so the model carries the window it was measured over and refuses bars from outside it.

One round turn a day costs 1.3% to 1.7% a year before a strategy has done anything at all. Twenty costs 26% to 35%. Turnover, not the per-trade figure, is what turns a half-basis-point cost into the dominant term in a backtest.

Limits

What this model does not cover

  • Market impact The feed carries no size, so there is no way to measure how a larger order moves the price. The parameter exists and defaults to zero; at retail size that is probably right, and at any size where it is not, this is the wrong tool.
  • Rejections and requotes Every order is modelled as filling. On market execution the practical equivalent is a worse fill, which the slippage term absorbs only in expectation.
  • Overnight swap Published per symbol and not included here. Anything holding a position past 21:00 UTC needs it added.
  • Two inferred contract sizes Gold's 100 oz lot and USTEC's single index point are inferred from the commission schedule, not published. Every pip-denominated figure on this page scales linearly with them, and both are worth confirming against the account's own contract specification.
